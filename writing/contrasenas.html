Password Managers and 2FA Authentication

CATEGORY: CYBERSECURITY / 2FA

[ ↓ RAW .ODT ]

Traditional internet authentication (relying on remembering one short password for multiple sites) works in practice like leaving a copy of your house key on the door of every business you visit. If a single site holding your password suffers a data breach, attackers try that same email-and-password combination across hundreds of services automatically (credential stuffing attacks). Trusting human memory or storing credentials in your web browser exposes your digital identity to account theft, impersonation and irreversible financial loss.

Adopting an open source password manager, combined with two-factor authentication (2FA) and physical security keys, means replacing this scheme with zero-knowledge cryptographic security, where every account has a unique, impossible-to-guess access credential, backed by hardware that only you possess.

Password Managers

An open source password manager is an application that works like an encrypted vault. Instead of memorizing dozens of passwords, you only remember one strong master password with which you enter the vault. The manager generates high-complexity random passwords (e.g. k9#mP$2x!L9qZ) for each service.

A good password manager has an encrypted vault and phishing protection. Your data is encrypted on your device before being sent to any server. The platform provider or your own self-hosted server can never read your keys or access your vault. Phishing protection recognizes the exact URL of the site; if you land on a fake page impersonating your bank, the manager will not autofill your credentials because the domain does not match, neutralizing the trick.

Two-Factor Authentication (2FA): The Second Line of Defense

2FA requires two elements to log in: something you know (your password) and something you have (a temporary code or a physical device).

There are different technologies for implementing 2FA, with very different security levels:

  1. SMS (Vulnerable): Codes sent by text message can be intercepted via SIM swapping or attacks on telephone networks. They should be avoided whenever possible.
  2. Time-based One-Time Password (TOTP) authenticator apps: They generate 6-digit codes that change every 30 seconds locally on your phone from a secret key. It is an accessible alternative and significantly more secure than SMS.
  3. Hardware Security Keys (FIDO2 / WebAuthn): The gold standard in digital security. It is a physical USB/NFC device that you must physically touch to authorize access. It is mathematically immune to phishing, because the key cryptographically validates the domain before granting access.

An integrated authentication strategy offers immunity against mass breaches, since if a site where you are registered is compromised, attackers obtain a credential that does not work on any of your other accounts; in addition, it provides total control over access, because physical hardware keys prevent someone from logging into your main email account or password manager remotely, even if they know your master password, since they do not possess the physical device. Likewise, it guarantees sovereignty and portability: by using free software and open formats, your credential databases are yours and you can export them, back them up or host them on your own servers at any time, without being trapped in proprietary ecosystems.

How do I use a password manager?

The simplest and most reliable recommendation is to use Bitwarden. It has applications for Android, iOS, Windows, macOS and Linux, plus extensions for the major browsers.

First, create an account with your email address and a long, unique master password that is easy for you to remember but hard for others to guess. This will be the only password you need to remember, so it is important not to reuse it on any other service and, if possible, store it in a safe place.

Then, install Bitwarden on your devices and set it as your default password manager. This way you can save, fill in and generate passwords directly from your apps and websites. If you already have passwords saved in another manager or in your browser, you can import them following the official instructions.

Once you have imported your passwords, change any that are weak, repeated or reused. The recommendation is to use a different, random password for each account. Bitwarden can generate and store them automatically.

Finally, enable two-factor authentication on your Bitwarden account and keep the recovery codes safe. This adds an extra layer of protection in case someone discovers your master password.

How do I use a TOTP app?

There are several applications for generating TOTP codes. If you need to sync them across multiple devices, you can use Bitwarden Authenticator. This option works with the same account as the Bitwarden password manager. If you prefer to keep your authentication codes separate from your passwords, you can use Ente Auth. Another alternative is Stratum Auth, which stores keys locally on an Android device, with no cloud sync.

After installing the app, log in to the account you want to protect and look in its settings for an option like “Two-factor authentication”, “2FA” or “Authenticator app”. Not all platforms offer this feature.

When you enable two-factor authentication, the service will show a QR code or a setup key. Scan the QR code with your TOTP app or enter the key manually. The app will store that key and start generating temporary codes, usually six digits long and valid for about 30 seconds.

The service will ask you to enter one of those codes to confirm the setup was done correctly. After that, every time you log in, in addition to your password you will have to enter the code generated by the app.

Also save the recovery codes the service provides and keep them in a safe place. These codes can help you regain access if you lose your device. Do not share the QR code, the setup key or the recovery codes with anyone, as they would allow your TOTP codes to be generated.

This way, even if someone discovers or steals your password, they will still need the second factor to access your account.

How do I use a physical security key?

A physical security key is a device you can use as a second authentication factor to protect your accounts. Some connect via USB, while others also work over NFC or Bluetooth. The most recommended ones use standards like FIDO2 or WebAuthn, since they offer solid protection against credential theft and fake websites.

To start, you need a compatible key and an account that supports security keys. Go to the security settings of the service you want to protect and look for an option like “Two-factor authentication”, “Security key”, “Hardware Security key”, but not “Passkey”, as those tend to be digital keys tied to proprietary Google or Microsoft applications. Not all platforms offer this feature.

Select the option to add a new key and follow the instructions. You may have to plug the key into your device via USB, bring it close via NFC, or press the key's own button or sensor to confirm it is you. Some models may also ask you for a PIN.

Then, give it a name that lets you identify it, such as “Main key” or “Work key” or the brand and model name. When you are done, log in to the account to check that it works correctly. From that point on, in addition to your password, the service may ask you to plug in or bring the key close to confirm access.

It is recommended to register at least two security keys on each important account: one to carry with you and another kept in a safe place as a backup. If you only register one and lose it, you could be locked out of the account.

You should also save the recovery codes offered by the service and keep other recovery methods up to date. Never share your key, its PIN or the recovery codes with other people. The key does not need to stay connected permanently: it is normally only used when logging in or making important changes to the account.

A security key adds a very strong layer of protection because, even if someone gets your password, they will normally also need to physically possess the key to gain access.

Recommended Tools for Privacy and Security

Password Managers

  • Bitwarden: A cross-platform, open source manager with zero-knowledge encryption. It allows using the official hosted service or installing your own instance with compatible solutions.
  • Vaultwarden: An alternative server, lightweight and compatible with the official Bitwarden clients. It is designed for those who want to self-host their own password manager locally.
  • KeePassXC: A fully offline manager. It stores the database locally in an encrypted file on your computer, with no need for external servers.

Two-Factor Authenticators (TOTP)

  • Bitwarden Authenticator: A cross-platform app for generating TOTP codes. It is integrated with the Bitwarden ecosystem and can sync codes across devices.
  • Stratum Auth: An open source Android app that stores TOTP keys locally on the device, with no cloud sync.
  • 979 2FA: An open source Android app focused on local storage of TOTP codes. It is available on Accrescent.

Hardware Security Keys (FIDO2)

  • SoloKeys and Nitrokey: Security keys with a strong open source focus. They are compatible with various FIDO2 and WebAuthn-based authentication methods.
  • Thetis: FIDO2 and WebAuthn compatible security keys, available with USB-A, USB-C and, depending on the model, NFC connections.
  • YubiKey - 5 and FIPS series: Durable physical keys widely compatible with services that support FIDO2/WebAuthn. Models with USB-A, USB-C and NFC are available.

Quick recommendations

  • For most users: Bitwarden.
  • To store passwords strictly locally: KeePassXC.
  • To keep TOTP codes separate from passwords: Stratum Auth or 979 2FA.
  • For maximum phishing protection: A physical FIDO2/WebAuthn key.
  • To have a physical backup: Register two security keys on your most important accounts.
